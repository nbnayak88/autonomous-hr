# Artificial Intelligence in HR — Panel Discussion 2
## Mega HR Conclave 2026 | Faridabad, India
**Theme:** AI in HR  
**Context:** Notes and synthesis from the second panel discussion at Mega HR Conclave 2026, held at Manav Rachna University with NHRDN Delhi & NCR Chapter.

> **Editorial note:** This document consolidates attendee notes and transcript excerpts. Statements attributed to speakers reflect the notes provided; figures and company examples have not been independently verified. Interpretive frameworks and action prompts are marked as synthesis.

## Executive summary

The panel's central message was that AI in HR must move **from experimentation and tool deployment to measurable business transformation**. Automating an existing process can improve speed, but deeper value requires redesigning work, roles, capabilities and the operating model around clearly defined outcomes.

The CHRO should help shape enterprise AI strategy—not merely oversee AI tools within HR. That includes anticipating how work will change, planning talent and reskilling, leading organizational change, and safeguarding privacy, fairness, transparency and human accountability.

A recurring principle was **AI can advise, accelerate and analyse; people remain accountable for consequential decisions**. The goal is not “AI versus people” but “AI with people,” with human judgment, critical thinking and employee dignity retained.

## 1. From experimentation to business expectations

AI has moved from experimentation to expectations. HR leaders are increasingly expected to explain the business problem being solved and the value created—not simply report that a process is faster or that a tool has been deployed.

### Automation versus transformation

- **Automation:** An existing process runs faster or with fewer manual steps.
- **Transformation:** The organization redesigns work, roles, decisions, capability and the operating model to address a meaningful business problem.
- **Business value:** Evidence that the change improves outcomes such as productivity, employee experience, customer experience, quality or time to productivity.

A 30% faster process, for example, is an efficiency indicator—not by itself proof that the underlying business problem has been solved.

### Workforce redesign example shared in the discussion

Chanchal Arora described a possible workforce-planning scenario in which an organization might redesign a portion of roles, reskill another portion of employees and recruit externally for remaining capability needs. The illustrative proportions mentioned were 20% role redesign, 30% reskilling and 50% external hiring. Treat these as an example used to explain workforce planning, **not a universal model or a forecast**.

The important questions are:
1. Which business activities should be automated, augmented or redesigned?
2. Which roles will change, disappear or emerge?
3. What capabilities will the future operating model require?
4. Can existing employees be reskilled or redeployed?
5. What outcomes will demonstrate that the transformation worked?

## 2. The CHRO's role in enterprise AI strategy

Lokendar Kumar described four roles for the CHRO in enterprise-wide AI transformation.

### 2.1 Architect of the people dimension
- Participate in enterprise decisions about what work should be transformed through AI.
- Assess which activities will disappear, emerge, or be augmented by AI.
- Bring workforce, role, culture and employee implications into decisions alongside technology and business considerations.

### 2.2 Talent strategist
- Revisit organization structures, competency models, career pathways and learning architecture.
- Identify new combinations of domain expertise, technology fluency, critical thinking, judgment and human skills.
- Connect workforce planning and capability investment to the future operating model.

### 2.3 Change leader
- Address employee questions about job security, relevance, algorithmic evaluation and changing expectations.
- Explain the purpose of AI adoption and how roles may be redesigned.
- Build transparency, participation, trust and readiness rather than treating adoption as a technology rollout alone.

### 2.4 Ethical guardian
- Establish governance for sensitive personal, performance and behavioural data.
- Address bias, privacy, security, transparency, explainability and accountability.
- Keep human oversight in consequential HR decisions, including hiring, selection, performance and career-impacting decisions.
- Define review, audit and escalation mechanisms for AI-supported processes.

**Core shift:** Move the conversation from “What will AI do to my people?” to “What can our people accomplish with AI?”

## 3. Redesign the work before deploying AI

An IBM experience shared during the panel described HR digitization beginning well before the generative AI wave, including an “Ask HR” capability. The speaker noted that digitizing HR operations did not remove HR professionals; it changed the work they could focus on, creating more room for judgment and higher-value contributions.

The speaker defined enterprise transformation as changing the way the organization works: its operating model, value creation, decision-making, work flows and how people grow. It is broader than implementing a software product or digitizing one function.

A useful work-design lens is to classify activities into three groups:

| Work pattern | Design question | HR implication |
|---|---|---|
| Human-led | Where are empathy, judgment, negotiation or accountability essential? | Preserve clear human ownership. |
| Human + AI | Where can AI support analysis, drafting, search or recommendations? | Define review steps and responsibility. |
| AI-agent-led | Which bounded, repeatable activities can agents perform safely? | Establish permissions, monitoring, auditability and escalation. |

The panel referenced external examples involving AI agents in manufacturing and AI models in steel production to illustrate transformation beyond HR. These were examples cited by the speaker, not independently verified here.

**Design principle:** Deploying AI on top of poorly designed processes may preserve existing friction. Reconsider the process, role and decision flow first, then determine where AI belongs.

## 4. Measuring outcomes rather than deployments

The number of tools deployed is not a sufficient measure of success. Establish a baseline, define target outcomes, and assess results after implementation.

Potential outcome measures include:

- Productivity and cycle time.
- Quality, accuracy and rework.
- Employee experience and service satisfaction.
- Customer experience where HR-enabled changes affect customers.
- Time to productivity for new employees.
- Capability improvement and application on the job.
- Adoption, trust and appropriate use.
- Fairness, privacy, security incidents and audit findings.

Use a balanced scorecard: productivity gains should not conceal deteriorating employee experience, unfair outcomes or increased risk.

### Learning and development example

Instead of asking only, “How many employees attended training?”, ask:
1. Which business capability was critical?
2. What performance or capability gap existed?
3. Which intervention addressed that gap?
4. Did people demonstrate the capability in real work?
5. Did the intervention improve the business outcome?

AI may help identify capability requirements, map skill gaps and suggest targeted interventions. Learning leaders still need to validate recommendations and check whether learning transfers into work.

## 5. Human judgment, overdependence and responsible AI

A key concern raised in the discussion was **overdependence on AI**. Overreliance can amplify other risks when people stop questioning the logic behind an output or treat a model's recommendation as a decision.

Risks highlighted included:
- Bias in historical data and downstream recommendations.
- Privacy and security exposure.
- Hallucinations or unsupported outputs.
- Loss of critical thinking and independent judgment.
- Unclear accountability for consequential decisions.
- Harm to employee opportunity, dignity or job security.

### Human-in-the-loop principles

1. **Question:** Ask what evidence and assumptions support the recommendation.
2. **Cross-check:** Verify important facts against reliable sources or systems of record.
3. **Test:** Evaluate outputs for accuracy, bias and performance across relevant groups.
4. **Decide:** Keep a qualified human accountable for high-impact decisions.
5. **Document:** Record the rationale, exceptions and approvals where appropriate.
6. **Monitor:** Review outcomes after deployment and revise or stop a system when risks emerge.

Recruitment was discussed as an area requiring particular care. Historical selection data can encode bias, so AI-generated interview questions, screening and shortlisting should not be accepted uncritically. Recruiters can use AI to support their work while retaining human evaluation and accountability.

## 6. HR technology and the employee experience

The panel emphasized that process is only one layer; the experience people have around the process matters too. AI should be evaluated not only by internal efficiency but also by the quality, accessibility and trustworthiness of the employee experience.

Useful questions:
- Does the solution make it easier for employees to get a reliable answer?
- Can employees understand when AI is involved and how to request human help?
- Is personal information handled appropriately?
- Are recommendations accessible and fair across employee groups?
- Does the system resolve the need or simply move the work elsewhere?

Technology adoption should strengthen human experience rather than undermine intuition, judgment, dignity or trust.

## 7. The future of Learning & Development

The discussion suggested that fixed training programs may become less relevant as work and skill requirements change faster. On-the-job learning, timely capability development and business-aligned interventions may become more important.

AI can help L&D teams:
- Identify emerging capability needs.
- Map role requirements to skills.
- Detect potential skill gaps.
- Suggest personalized learning or practice.
- Support learning in the flow of work.
- Evaluate whether capability development contributes to business results.

This does not mean all structured learning will disappear. It means programs should be designed around validated needs and outcomes, rather than attendance or course completion alone.

## 8. A practical framework for HR leaders

Use this sequence when evaluating an AI opportunity in HR or across the enterprise:

1. **Define the business problem.** Describe the issue and the people affected.
2. **Set the outcome.** Choose measurable business and people indicators.
3. **Understand the work.** Map activities, decisions, hand-offs and pain points.
4. **Redesign the process.** Remove unnecessary steps before automating.
5. **Choose the AI role.** Human-led, human-plus-AI, or bounded AI-agent-led.
6. **Plan the workforce.** Identify role changes, reskilling, redeployment and hiring needs.
7. **Govern the risks.** Set controls for data, bias, security, transparency and human review.
8. **Pilot and validate.** Test with users, check exceptions and compare against a baseline.
9. **Measure and learn.** Review outcomes, gather feedback and iterate.
10. **Scale responsibly.** Expand only when value and controls are demonstrated.

## 9. Questions for an applied HR industry lab

1. What specific business problem should an AI-enabled HR solution solve?
2. Is the proposed use case automation, augmentation or operating-model transformation?
3. What should be redesigned before AI is introduced?
4. Which activities require human judgment or empathy?
5. What roles and capabilities could change over the next three years?
6. How will HR determine whether to reskill, redeploy or recruit?
7. What data is necessary, and what data should not be used?
8. How will the solution be tested for bias and reliability?
9. Who is accountable when an AI recommendation leads to harm?
10. Which metrics will prove improvements in productivity and experience?
11. How will employees understand, question or appeal AI-supported outcomes?
12. What evidence is required before scaling the solution?

## 10. Key takeaways

- **Business problem before technology:** Start with the problem and intended value.
- **Transformation before automation:** Redesign work and processes instead of simply accelerating existing ones.
- **CHRO at the enterprise table:** People and capability implications must be considered when enterprise AI strategy is shaped.
- **Capability before attendance:** Measure applied skills and business impact, not only training activity.
- **AI with people:** Use technology to augment human capability, not to remove accountability.
- **Governance by design:** Protect privacy, fairness, security, transparency and employee dignity.
- **Human judgment remains essential:** Question, verify and take responsibility for consequential decisions.
- **Outcomes over tool counts:** AI becomes a business investment when benefits are demonstrated and risks managed.

## Closing reflection

> The question is not simply how much HR work AI can perform. The question is how intelligently we can redesign work so that people, AI and the enterprise create better outcomes together.
