## Executive summary

The panel explored how HR leadership is evolving as organisations adopt AI, redesign performance management, compete for talent and respond to changing employee expectations. The recurring message was that HR must understand the business and its people, build organisational capability, shape culture and use technology with judgement rather than accepting AI outputs uncritically.

A useful shorthand for the discussion:

**Business understanding + people insight + technology judgement + culture + delivery.**

## 1. The evolving role of HR leadership

The discussion raised the possibility that future leadership roles may increasingly emphasise capability and transformation—for example, a **Chief Capability and Transformation Officer**—rather than relying only on the traditional CHRO label.

This points to a broader shift: HR leadership is expected to contribute directly to business transformation, capability building and organisational performance.

### What future HR leaders need to do

- Understand the business model, operating context and value drivers.
- Interpret business challenges through a people lens.
- Connect talent, skills, leadership and culture to business outcomes.
- Work across functions and manage stakeholders effectively.
- Help the organisation adapt continuously, not only during formal change programmes.

## 2. HR remains relevant in an AI-enabled world

A key message attributed in the notes to Rajiv Kapoor was that HR remains relevant because technology does not remove the human impact of organisational decisions.

AI should be used as an aid to analysis—not accepted blindly.

### Practical principles for responsible AI use

1. **Analyse:** Understand the problem and the context before using an AI recommendation.
2. **Cross-check:** Verify generated insights against reliable data and real-world evidence.
3. **Apply judgement:** Use human experience, intuition and professional accountability.
4. **Evolve:** Learn how AI changes work and redesign workflows accordingly.
5. **Keep people in view:** Assess implications for employee experience, trust, fairness and capability.

AI-generated answers are inputs to decision-making, not substitutes for accountability.

## 3. Capability velocity and continuous performance management

The panel notes suggest a shift away from treating targets and periodic performance ratings as the only measures that matter. Capability development, learning agility and growth are becoming increasingly important.

### Implications for performance management

- Move from a once-a-year event towards continuous conversations and feedback.
- Connect individual contributions to business value drivers.
- Use AI to help identify potential value drivers for different roles, while validating recommendations with managers and employees.
- Automate suitable operational activities so HR and leaders can focus on higher-value work.
- Evaluate both outcomes and the capabilities needed to sustain future performance.

**Design consideration:** AI can suggest role-specific value drivers, but organisations should validate them for relevance, fairness, measurability and alignment with strategy. Avoid turning AI-generated suggestions into automatic performance judgements.

## 4. HR technology, agents and adoption

The discussion included the possibility that AI agents may become part of workflows overseen by HR. HR leaders therefore need to understand HR technology and have the courage to experiment with new approaches.

Technology adoption requires more than buying or deploying a tool.

- Identify a meaningful business or employee problem.
- Redesign the process and experience around the intended outcome.
- Define human oversight, accountability and escalation paths.
- Test the solution with users.
- Measure adoption and actual value delivered.
- Iterate based on evidence and feedback.

Technology should enhance human experience, not undermine it.

## 5. Culture: one word that matters most to HR

The panel's concise answer to the question of the most important word for HR was **Culture**.

Culture is not only a statement of values. It is reflected in everyday leadership behaviour, decision-making, incentives, collaboration and how employees experience the organisation.

HR can help shape culture by aligning:

- **Principles:** What the organisation believes and expects.
- **Practice:** How leaders and teams behave in real situations.
- **Process:** How systems, policies and workflows reinforce the intended behaviours.

When principles, practice and process conflict, employees often experience the gap more strongly than the stated values.

## 6. Loyalty, contribution and changing employee expectations

The notes capture a discussion about how employee loyalty is changing and how organisations should measure it.

One perspective shared by Manasi Das was to focus on **contribution rather than tenure alone**. Time spent with an organisation does not, by itself, establish the value or impact of a person's work.

This invites HR to consider questions such as:

- What meaningful contribution has the employee made?
- What capabilities have they built or shared?
- How have they helped customers, colleagues or business outcomes?
- What conditions would help them continue to grow and contribute?

Tenure can provide context, but it should not be used as a proxy for contribution.

## 7. Standardisation versus individual employee experience

Large organisations need consistency, governance and scalable processes. At the same time, employees have different needs, career aspirations and circumstances.

The panel discussion framed standardisation as a destination for core processes while allowing customised journeys where employee context matters.

### A practical design approach

- **Standardise the foundation:** Common policies, controls, data definitions and essential process steps.
- **Personalise the journey:** Adapt learning, career development, communication and support to relevant employee needs.
- **Use technology thoughtfully:** Apply AI and digital tools to make experiences more responsive, without assuming that every recommendation is correct.
- **Measure the experience:** Examine whether processes are understandable, accessible and useful—not merely whether they are compliant.

A process is a layer of the system; the employee experience extends beyond the process itself.

## 8. Attracting, retaining and developing talent

The discussion identified attracting and retaining talent as continuing challenges, with retention potentially more difficult than attraction.

Leadership potential should be developed in the context of real work. Employees often grow through their interactions with functional leaders, challenging assignments and cross-functional collaboration.

HR can contribute by:

- Identifying emerging leadership potential.
- Giving employees meaningful opportunities to practise leadership.
- Supporting functional leaders as talent developers.
- Building environments where people can learn, contribute and progress.
- Connecting career development with changing market and business needs.

## 9. When does HR become strategic?

The panel raised a familiar question: why do some HR functions become strategic partners while others remain primarily operational?

A practical answer emerging from the notes is that HR creates strategic value when it helps the business put the **right talent in the right place at the right time**, in ways that contribute to business outcomes.

This requires more than HR expertise. It calls for:

- Business and market understanding.
- Stakeholder management.
- Evidence-informed decisions.
- Cross-functional collaboration.
- Clear links between workforce actions and business results.

For a new-market entry, HR and the CEO would need to consider the talent model, leadership requirements, local context, workforce capability, organisational culture and the people-related risks of execution.

## 10. Future of Learning & Development

The discussion pointed towards a more market-driven and business-connected approach to learning and development.

Potential shifts include:

- From assessment alone to building capabilities needed by the business.
- From static individual development plans to learning connected with changing work.
- From a transactional L&D function to stronger business partnership.
- From passive content consumption to experiential decisions, practice and reflection.
- From assuming AI is correct to questioning, analysing and validating its recommendations.

Learning should help people handle real challenges, collaborate across functions and keep pace with change.

## 11. Multigenerational workforce and employee experience

A multigenerational workforce includes people with different career stages, expectations, experiences and preferences. Effective HR leadership should avoid assuming that age alone predicts an individual's needs.

Useful practices include:

- Listening to employees rather than relying on generational stereotypes.
- Offering appropriate flexibility in learning and career pathways.
- Encouraging mentoring and reverse mentoring.
- Creating inclusive collaboration across teams and levels.
- Maintaining common principles while adapting the support employees receive.

The goal is not to create a separate culture for every group, but to create a shared environment in which different people can contribute effectively.

## 12. Applied HR leadership framework

The panel's themes can be translated into a practical sequence for HR leaders:

1. **Understand the business:** Clarify strategy, market conditions and value drivers.
2. **Understand the context:** Identify operating constraints, stakeholders and workforce realities.
3. **Understand the people:** Listen to employees and leaders; examine capability, motivation and experience.
4. **Define the outcome:** State what needs to change and how success will be recognised.
5. **Design the intervention:** Align talent, process, technology, leadership and culture.
6. **Use AI with judgement:** Analyse, question, cross-check and retain accountable human decision-making.
7. **Enable adoption:** Communicate, coach, experiment and support behaviour change.
8. **Measure contribution:** Track business outcomes, capability growth and employee experience.
9. **Learn and adapt:** Use evidence and feedback to improve the approach.

## 13. Reflection questions for an HR industry lab

Use these questions for group discussion, a workshop or a scenario-based hackathon:

1. What business outcome should HR enable in this scenario?
2. Which capabilities will the organisation need over the next three to five years?
3. Where could AI add value, and where is human judgement essential?
4. How would you validate an AI-generated performance value driver?
5. Which parts of the employee journey should be standardised, and which should be personalised?
6. How can contribution be assessed without relying on tenure alone?
7. What would make a learning intervention market-driven and experiential?
8. How can functional leaders become active developers of leadership talent?
9. Which principles, practices and processes currently reinforce—or undermine—the desired culture?
10. What evidence would demonstrate that the proposed HR intervention was delivered and created value?

## Key takeaway

The future of HR leadership is not simply about adopting more technology or renaming the CHRO role. It is about understanding business and people, building capabilities at speed, shaping culture, improving employee experience and using AI with critical judgement.

**The test of HR's strategic value is whether it helps the organisation and its people achieve meaningful outcomes—and can demonstrate that value in practice.**
