# Mega HR Conclave 2026 — Keynote: The Future of Work, India’s Growth Opportunity, and HR’s Role

> **Session type:** Single-speaker keynote  
> **Event:** Mega HR Conclave 2026, Manav Rachna University, Faridabad  
> **Date:** 9 October 2026  
> **Documentation approach:** Anonymised, contextual learning notes based on the session transcript. Figures and forecasts below are presented as cited by the speaker, not as independently verified facts.

## Executive summary

The keynote placed HR’s future in a wider economic, technological, and demographic context. As the pace of change accelerates, the speaker argued that the question is no longer whether technology will affect work, but how organisations will combine human judgment and technology to create value.

The central message was optimistic but not complacent: India has a major growth opportunity, and HR can help organisations prepare by building digital and AI fluency, enabling reskilling, supporting organisational renewal, developing resilience, and protecting the human capabilities that technology cannot replace by itself—such as judgment, relationships, culture, creativity, trust, and leadership.

**Core idea:** The future belongs to organisations that can reinvent themselves quickly while keeping people at the centre of technological change.

## 1. The pace of change is accelerating

The speaker described successive eras of human enterprise—from agriculture and feudal systems through mercantile activity and industrialisation to digital and AI-enabled work. The duration of these eras has shortened over time.

Two shifts follow:

- **Change cycles are becoming shorter.** Organisations cannot rely on static strategies, fixed skills, or slow transformation cycles.
- **The relationship between people and machines is changing.** Earlier waves of technology often emphasised replacing human effort with machines. The emerging model increasingly involves people and intelligent systems working together.

For HR, this requires continuous learning, workforce planning that anticipates change, and operating models that can adapt as work evolves.

## 2. Geopolitics and the changing global economic landscape

The keynote connected business decisions to geopolitical uncertainty, conflict, changing supply chains, and a growing role for developing economies. The speaker highlighted the “China plus one” strategy and argued that India is positioned to benefit as organisations reconsider manufacturing and supply-chain dependencies.

The strategic implication for HR is to look beyond current vacancies and traditional sectors:

- Identify emerging industries and capability needs.
- Build talent pipelines for areas such as semiconductors, electronics, defence, space, renewable energy, healthcare, and digital services.
- Help leaders understand the workforce implications of investment and location decisions.
- Treat uncertainty as a reason to improve scenario planning—not as a reason to stop investing in capabilities.

These were presented as opportunities and directional trends; specific forecasts and sector outcomes should be validated against current evidence before being used in business cases.

## 3. India’s growth opportunity and demographic dividend

The speaker described India’s potential economic expansion through 2050 and highlighted the country’s working-age population as a strategic advantage, with the demographic dividend expected to be particularly favourable around 2030.

The keynote cited several projections, including:

- India potentially becoming a US$5 trillion economy by 2028.
- India’s economy growing several-fold between the present and 2050.
- The top 20 economies collectively increasing substantially in size by 2050.

These are **speaker-cited projections**, not guaranteed outcomes. Their assumptions, dates, and latest official estimates should be checked before being reused in presentations or planning documents.

### What this means for HR

Economic growth does not automatically translate into organisational success. The opportunity must be converted into workforce capability through:

1. Skills intelligence linked to business strategy.
2. Reskilling and internal mobility.
3. Partnerships with universities, vocational providers, and industry.
4. Workforce planning for new and expanding sectors.
5. Stronger connections between learning investment and business outcomes.

HR should participate in strategic discussions about where the business intends to grow—and what people, skills, leadership, and culture will make that growth possible.

## 4. Digital and AI fluency are becoming essential capabilities

The speaker argued that digitalisation is no longer limited to technology companies. Traditional businesses—including manufacturing and materials companies—are increasingly using digital platforms to run important parts of their operations.

Therefore, digital literacy and AI fluency should be treated as core workforce capabilities rather than optional additions.

### Practical implications

- Define AI and digital fluency by role, not only through a generic organisation-wide course.
- Help employees identify appropriate use cases and understand limitations.
- Provide opportunities to practise with real work scenarios.
- Teach verification, privacy, security, bias awareness, and responsible handling of sensitive information.
- Measure whether the learning improves work quality, decision-making, service, or productivity—not just course completion.

AI fluency should include the ability to decide when **not** to use AI.

## 5. AI, jobs, and the redesign of work

The keynote acknowledged that AI will displace some jobs and tasks while creating new work. It cited a projection of **170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced by 2030**, producing a net gain in that estimate. This figure should be attributed to the research referenced by the speaker and verified against the original report before external publication.

The speaker compared current concerns about AI with earlier debates about computerisation. The important question is not simply whether technology will be adopted, but whether workers can learn to use it effectively.

### From task automation to work redesign

The keynote suggested that repetitive and transactional tasks are strong candidates for automation, potentially freeing people to focus more on expertise and relationships.

A responsible implementation should distinguish between:

- **Task automation:** Making an existing activity faster or less manual.
- **Role redesign:** Changing how responsibilities are distributed between people, software, and AI.
- **Business transformation:** Changing workflows, services, decisions, or the operating model to create a different level of value.

Automation alone does not guarantee transformation. Organisations must redesign work, manage transitions, reskill affected employees, and monitor the quality and consequences of the new process.

### HR’s role

- Map tasks and skills rather than assuming entire jobs will disappear.
- Assess which activities require empathy, accountability, judgment, contextual knowledge, or human interaction.
- Plan redeployment and reskilling alongside automation.
- Involve employees in redesigning the work they understand best.
- Establish clear human accountability for consequential decisions.

## 6. People-first technology adoption

The speaker contrasted a technology-first approach with a people-first approach, citing research that reportedly found better outcomes when people remain at the centre of technology adoption. A figure of **1.6 times better outcomes** was mentioned in the keynote; the underlying study and its methodology should be verified before the statistic is reused.

The durable principle is that technology creates value when it improves human and organisational outcomes—not when adoption becomes an end in itself.

A people-centred transformation considers:

- Employee experience and accessibility.
- The impact on roles, workload, and autonomy.
- Trust, communication, and participation.
- Skills and support required to use new tools.
- Privacy, fairness, safety, and accountability.
- Whether the technology solves a real problem.

## 7. Human judgment must not be outsourced to AI

The speaker observed that people increasingly turn to AI assistants for answers and raised a critical question: **Are we also outsourcing our thinking?**

AI can help people explore options, summarise information, generate ideas, and accelerate routine work. However, users still need to question outputs, check evidence, understand context, and take responsibility for decisions.

### A practical human-in-the-loop discipline

1. **Frame:** Define the actual problem and the decision to be made.
2. **Ask:** Use AI to generate options, questions, or a first draft.
3. **Verify:** Check important facts, assumptions, calculations, and sources.
4. **Challenge:** Look for missing context, bias, uncertainty, and alternative explanations.
5. **Decide:** Apply professional judgment and organisational values.
6. **Own:** Keep accountability with the appropriate human decision-maker.

This is especially important in HR decisions involving hiring, performance, pay, employee relations, promotion, health, or other sensitive personal information.

## 8. Multigenerational workforces and reverse mentoring

The keynote highlighted the continued presence of multiple generations in the workforce. Rather than treating generational diversity as a problem to solve, organisations can use it as a source of complementary experience and capability.

The speaker recommended considering **reverse mentoring**, where younger employees share their digital practices and technology fluency with more experienced colleagues, while experienced professionals contribute business context, judgment, and organisational knowledge.

Effective reverse mentoring should be reciprocal rather than based on stereotypes. Individuals differ, and capability should be understood through actual skills and experience—not assumptions about age or generation.

Possible practices include:

- Cross-generational learning pairs.
- Digital and AI practice sessions led by employees with relevant expertise.
- Knowledge-sharing circles around real business challenges.
- Mentoring that flows in both directions.
- Recognition for teaching, learning, and collaboration across experience levels.

## 9. Intangible assets are a major source of organisational value

A key part of the keynote contrasted tangible assets—such as plants, buildings, machinery, and products—with intangible assets, including talent, culture, reputation, creativity, intellectual capital, and relationships.

The speaker presented a historical series attributed to research on S&P 500 companies, suggesting that the relative contribution of intangible assets to market value had grown significantly over time, with a figure of approximately **92%** mentioned for 2025.

**Validation note:** The exact percentages, years, definitions, and research source were not established in the transcript. Treat the series as a keynote claim until the original research is identified and verified. Market value is influenced by many factors, and the figure should not be interpreted as a universal causal measure of HR’s contribution.

### The strategic message for HR

HR has an opportunity to influence assets that are difficult to copy and central to long-term performance:

- Organisational culture and leadership behaviour.
- Workforce capability and learning agility.
- Employee trust, engagement, and relationships.
- Creativity, innovation, and collaboration.
- Reputation and the employee experience.
- The ability to adapt when the business environment changes.

HR can strengthen its strategic position by linking these factors to observable business outcomes rather than presenting them only as people metrics.

## 10. Risks leaders must manage

The speaker closed by warning against several risks that accompany rapid technological change.

### Adaptability risk
Organisations may be too slow to change their processes, capabilities, and assumptions.

**HR response:** Build continuous learning into work, strengthen internal mobility, and practise scenario planning.

### Linear-thinking risk
Leaders may assume the future will be a simple continuation of current trends, even when systems are complex and change is nonlinear.

**HR response:** Use scenarios, test assumptions, consider second-order effects, and keep options open.

### Hype risk
Organisations may overestimate what a technology can do or adopt it because of external pressure.

**HR response:** Start with a defined business problem, run bounded experiments, evaluate evidence, and scale only when results justify it.

### Overdependence risk
People may accept AI outputs without checking them or gradually lose the habit of independent reasoning.

**HR response:** Build critical thinking, source verification, and human accountability into AI learning and operating procedures.

### Resilience and wellbeing risk
The speaker raised concerns about how employees—particularly those early in their careers—will cope with setbacks, competition, uncertainty, and failure. The keynote linked this to resilience, emotional steadiness, mindfulness, and mental wellness.

These concerns should not be generalised to an entire generation. Resilience varies by individual and is shaped by leadership, work design, psychological safety, support systems, and the environment employees experience.

**HR response:** Build resilience through constructive feedback, meaningful responsibility, supportive managers, recovery from setbacks, mental-wellbeing resources, and a culture where asking for help is safe.

## 11. An applied HR transformation agenda

The keynote can be translated into a practical action agenda.

### First 30 days — Understand

- Identify the business priorities and growth areas most likely to affect workforce needs.
- Map major workflows and distinguish transactional tasks from work requiring human expertise.
- Establish baseline measures for digital and AI fluency.
- Identify high-impact risks involving privacy, bias, accuracy, employee experience, and accountability.
- Listen to employees and managers about friction points and capability gaps.

### Days 31–60 — Experiment

- Select a small number of well-defined AI or digital use cases.
- Co-design pilots with employees, managers, IT, risk, and relevant business teams.
- Provide role-specific learning and guided practice.
- Test the quality of outputs and the impact on work, not just speed.
- Launch cross-generational mentoring or peer-learning experiments.

### Days 61–90 — Evaluate and scale responsibly

- Compare results with the baseline and intended business outcomes.
- Review quality, employee experience, adoption, risks, and unintended effects.
- Decide which experiments to scale, redesign, or stop.
- Update role profiles, learning journeys, and workforce plans where needed.
- Communicate what was learned and how employee feedback changed the approach.

## 12. Measures that make the agenda observable

Choose measures appropriate to each use case rather than relying on a single AI-adoption metric.

| Focus area | Example measures |
|---|---|
| Digital and AI fluency | Demonstrated proficiency, quality of completed scenarios, safe-use compliance |
| Productivity | Cycle time, manual effort, rework, service quality |
| Workforce transition | Skills gained, internal mobility, redeployment, time to proficiency |
| Employee experience | User satisfaction, trust, accessibility, feedback themes |
| Decision quality | Error rates, override patterns, documented human review |
| Organisational adaptability | Time to implement change, experiment-to-learning cycle, adoption of validated practices |
| Culture and leadership | Manager effectiveness, collaboration, psychological safety, employee feedback |
| Business value | Outcomes tied to the original business case, with costs and risks included |

## 13. Reflection questions for HR professionals

1. Which strategic business decisions should HR influence more actively over the next three years?
2. Where are we automating tasks without rethinking the underlying process?
3. Which workforce capabilities will become essential as our industry digitises?
4. How do we ensure that AI augments judgment instead of replacing critical thinking?
5. What would a meaningful people-first technology implementation look like in our organisation?
6. How can reverse mentoring and reciprocal learning work across levels and generations?
7. Which intangible assets—culture, trust, talent, reputation, or creativity—are most important to our strategy?
8. How do we build resilience without normalising excessive pressure or poor management?
9. What evidence would convince us to scale, change, or stop an AI experiment?
10. What should HR start doing differently in the next 90 days?

## 14. Applied industry-lab challenge

**Challenge:** Design a people-centred AI-enabled workflow that improves a measurable business outcome without weakening human judgment, employee trust, or accountability.

Participants should:

1. Select a real HR or business workflow.
2. Map the current process, users, pain points, and risks.
3. Identify tasks suitable for automation and tasks requiring human judgment.
4. Define a small, testable intervention.
5. Specify the human review and escalation points.
6. Create a role-based learning and change plan.
7. Define baseline measures and success criteria.
8. Run a tabletop test using normal, edge, and failure scenarios.
9. Recommend whether to scale, revise, or stop the intervention.
10. Document lessons and the next experiment.

**Expected output:** A one-page architecture of the workflow, a human-versus-AI responsibility map, a risk checklist, a learning plan, and a measurable pilot scorecard.

## Closing synthesis

The keynote reframed the future of HR as an opportunity to shape organisational renewal—not simply to respond to automation. India’s economic and demographic prospects may create significant possibilities, but organisations will need the capabilities to convert those possibilities into sustained value.

The strongest HR response combines digital and AI fluency with critical thinking, business understanding, culture, resilience, and a deliberate commitment to people-centred change. Technology should expand what people can achieve, while people remain responsible for the choices that matter.

**Takeaway:** Reinvention is not a one-time transformation programme. It is an organisational capability—built through learning, experimentation, human judgment, and the willingness to adapt when the future changes.
